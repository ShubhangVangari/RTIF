01

What this is

A knowledge base built from 152 documents about LLM engineering — evaluation, retrieval, hallucination, long context, and agents. Roughly 120 arXiv papers and 30 practitioner blog posts and framework docs.

The practitioner sources were chosen deliberately: they argue with each other. One post is called “Don't Build Multi-Agents.” Another is a case study of a multi-agent system running in production. Both are in here.

That disagreement is the point.

02

How it's built

Most retrieval systems chop documents into chunks, turn each chunk into a list of numbers, and at question time find the closest chunks by arithmetic. Nothing reads anything until the answer is being written.

This one works differently. Every document was read by a model and compiled into wiki pages — 31 of them, one per concept. When a new document said something a page already covered, it was folded in. When it said something a page contradicted, both positions were recorded side by side.

To answer a question, a model reads an index of all 31 page summaries, decides which pages to open, reads them, and can follow a link to open another. Like using a library catalogue rather than a search engine.

The cost of doing it this way
Compiling took about 5 hours and $25. The vector system indexed the same documents in 7 seconds for 2 cents.
What you get for it
Pages a person can read, where every claim names its source and disagreements are written down rather than left to chance.
03

Try it

Ask the same question of both systems. Use the side-by-side toggle. The answers are usually both reasonable — the difference is in what each one can show you.
Click a citation in a wiki answer. The page opens. You can read the claim in its full context, see what else the corpus says about that topic, and check whether sources disagree.
Try clicking a citation in the vector answer. You can't. It cites DOC-087 — a document id for a chunk of text. There's no page to open.
Watch the trace under a wiki answer. It shows every step: which pages it opened, whether it followed a link, whether it asked for something twice. The vector system has no trace, because nothing was decided — arithmetic picked five chunks before the model was involved.
Ask about something the corpus doesn't cover — speculative decoding, model quantization, federated learning. Vector search always returns its five closest chunks, however irrelevant. This one can tell you it doesn't know.
Look for the contested marker in the index. Those six pages hold genuine conflict between sources. Open one and read the Disagreement section at the bottom.
04

What the comparison found

Both systems were asked the same 30 questions, sorted into six types, and graded blind by a third model.

The vector system scored higher overall — 21.5 to 20. That's the honest result, and it's the right one. The two are good at different questions.

Where the vector system wins: anything needing the exact text. Compiling is lossy — the wiki kept the benchmark names and lost the numbers. Raw chunks keep them verbatim.

Where this one wins: relationships. A disagreement between two documents isn't in either document — it only exists once someone writes it down. The clearest case: one source argues a minority position against seventeen on the other side. Top-five similarity never surfaced it. The wiki has it on the page because the compiler had to decide what to do with it when it arrived.

What it costs: about three times the money and three times the wait per question.

06

Known limitations

Once asked about a topic that is covered, the wiki answered “not covered” — because the page's one-line summary didn't mention it, and the summary is all that selection sees.
About 15% of sentences across the pages have no source cited. That's the model writing connective text of its own on pages that claim full provenance.
The compile run was stopped at 152 of 223 documents: cost per document kept climbing as pages grew.
New pages can't be created. The rule requires two independent sources, but the check runs while looking at one document at a time — so it can never pass.
